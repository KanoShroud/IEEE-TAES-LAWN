Direct Trajectory Estimation of Moving Emitters
With Distributed Stations and Performance
Analysis to Station Location Errors
Jinlong Ren , Guohua Wei , Senior Member, IEEE, Xiaojie Bao, Jiahao Bai , and Xu Wang
Abstract—For trajectory estimation of moving emitters,
we propose a one-step method based on widely distributed
stations. Benefiting from the distributed multistation geom-
etry, the proposed method makes full use of the potential
Doppler frequency, angle and time delay information to
determine the emitter trajectory. Compared with traditional
methods, this approach combines measurement extraction
and trajectory estimation in a single step, yielding a superior
solution. To obtain a theoretical benchmark, we derive cor-
responding Cramér–Rao bounds with compact-form for the
interested trajectory parameters. Moreover, considering the
effects of the station location errors, we establish the mathematical relations between the station location errors and the
deviation of the trajectory for the moving emitter. Finally, numerical simulations are conducted to verify the superiority
of the proposed method and the correctness of the theoretical analysis.
Index Terms— Distributed stations, location error, perturbation analysis, trajectory estimation.
I. INTRODUCTION
T
ARGET trajectory estimation plays a key role in various
applications, including underwater navigation [1], [2],
range measurement [3], [4], and radio emitter reconnais-
sance [5], [6]. Passive systems for target trajectory estimation
have significant advantages over active manners, such as the
ability to counter stealth technology, reduced vulnerability to
electronic counter-measures, and lower cost [7], [8]. This arti-
cle especially focuses on the trajectory estimation of moving
radio emitters in passive multistation systems.
Traditionally, passive localization and tracking of emitters
follows a two-step procedure. First, location measurements
related to the emitters’ position and velocity are extracted from
the received signals at each observation station independently.
The common location measurements include angle of arrival
(AOA), time difference of arrival (TDOA), and frequency
difference of arrival (FDOA) [9], [10], [11]. Then, these
location measurements are fused together in a central proces-

sor by solving a set of location equations. Due to the high
nonlinearity between the location measurements and tar-
get parameters, there have been developed several popular
algorithms, such as weighed least squares (WLSs) [12], pseu-
dolinear estimation (PLE) [13], [14], Bayesian filtering [15],
and convex optimization [16]. Although these two-step meth-
ods could locate and track emitters, there inherently exist some
limitations. First, the location measurements are extracted
independently, which overlooks the essential constraint where
these location measurements originate from the same emit-
ter. Second, when these measurements are used together for
localization, their precise probability distribution information
is challenging to obtain [17]. The suboptimal weighted factors
used will degrade the performance of target localization.
In summary, achieving satisfactory estimation accuracy for
emitter localization and tracking following a two-step proce-
dure is challenging.
Recently, a one-step method, also known as direct posi-
tion determination (DPD) has emerged to localize and track
emitters. In contrast to the two-step procedure, DPD directly
searches for the optimal solution from the positioning param-
eter space using the raw received signals [18]. Theoretically,
DPD offers the potential for improved performance by using
as much location information as possible and avoiding infor-
mation loss in a step-by-step procedure efficiently.
Among the numerous literature about DPD, the main focus
is on stationary emitter localization. Therein, considering

developed corresponding algorithms based on the maximum
likelihood principle. The other researches were devoted to
localize multiple emitters based on spatial spectrum. To better
distinguish closely spaced emitters, several DPD-based estima-
tors have been developed, such as MUSIC-type, MVDR-type,
and their variants [19], [20], [21].
For moving emitters, they are often more threatening,
especially in military applications. Therefore, localization and
tracking of moving emitters are of greater significance. The
above-mentioned DPD-based methods for stationary emitters
cannot be applicable to moving emitters. Nevertheless, some
literature discussed the problem of localization and tracking
of moving emitters. Sidi and Weiss [22] deployed multiple
separated observation stations with a single antenna to local-
ize and track the moving emitter, where Doppler frequency
and TOA were utilized. Afterward, Wang and Wang [23]
focused on a moving narrowband emitter and formulated a
DPD-based estimator where only Doppler frequency is con-
sidered. In addition, Sun et al. [24] and Ma et al. [25] further
applied the Bayesian-based filter to track a moving emitter
directly. However, AOA information is not fully considered in
these developed algorithms for moving emitters. Undoubtedly,
adding the angle measurement ability of observation stations
can further improve the potential localization and tracking
accuracy of moving emitters.
In practical application scenarios, obtaining the precise
coordinates of observation stations is often challenging. For
example, these observation stations may be mounted on ships
or hovering drones, which inevitably introduces disturbances
to the observation station location due to the influence of
random sea swell and air currents. Ignoring these station
location errors may degrade the performance of emitter local-
ization and tracking. To address this problem, various studies
have been conducted. For the traditional two-step method,
Ho et al. [26] and Jia et al. [27] applied the pseudolinear
operation to mitigate the effects of the station location errors.
Besides these, Ren et al. [28] employed augmented variables
to estimate the actual observation station locations, thereby
compensating for the impacts of the location errors. However,
these methods are all aimed at the extracted measurements. For
DPD-based methods, the existing literature on station location
errors is designed predominantly for stationary emitters [29],
[30], [31]. In the case of the moving emitter’s localization, the
position and velocity should be considered together. Due to the
fact that the position and velocity are coupled in the DPD-
based estimator, the off-the-shelf conclusion and analysis for
stationary emitters are not applicable directly. The errors in
station location cause both drifts in the position and moving
direction for the moving emitters. The drift in the moving
direction will cause great accumulated location errors. As far
as we know, this issue has not been reported in published
literature. As a result, it is necessary to analyze the effects of
the station location errors on moving emitters.
In this article, we propose a DPD-based method using
widely distributed observation stations to determine the trajec-
tory of a 3-D moving emitter, in which the AOA, TDOA, and
FDOA information are jointly utilized. In addition, we analyze
the effects of location errors in observation stations on the
trajectory of moving emitters (i.e., the drifts in position and
moving direction). To the best of our knowledge, this particular
approach has not been studied in existing literature.
The main contributions in this article are summarized as
follows.
1) We propose a one-step method for trajectory estimation
of 3-D moving emitters, which uses the most com-
plete location information including AOA, TDOA, and
FDOA. The proposed method inherits the advantages
of the DPD-based method and demonstrates superior
performance in trajectory parameters estimation.
2) We conduct theoretical performance analysis to location
errors in observation stations for moving emitters and
provide closed-form expressions for the drifts in the
estimated trajectory parameters.
The rest of the article is organized as follows. In Section II,
we formulate the signal model for a 3-D moving emitter
utilizing widely distributed and stationary stations. Section III
develops a one-step method for trajectory estimation of a
3-D moving emitter, derives a compact form of the CRB
and discusses the computation complexity and implementation
suggestions of the proposed method. Then, the performance
analysis of station location errors for the proposed method is
analyzed in Section IV. Section V provides some simulations
to verify the superiority of the proposed method and the cor-
rectness of the performance analysis. Finally, some concluding
remarks are provided in Section VI.
II. SIGNAL MODEL
Consider the location scenario where L widely distributed
observation stations are deployed to intercept the radiated
signal from the moving emitter. Each station consists of
M antenna elements. All intercepted signals at each station
are transmitted to the central data processor to estimate the
trajectory of the moving emitter, as depicted in Fig. 1. The
time and frequency synchronized between observation stations
is assumed to be achieved in advance [32], [33]. When the
emitter moves in a stable state, the transition noise can be rea-
sonably considered negligible. Following [17], [34], and [35],
we assume that the emitter moves evenly in a straight line.
The geographic coordinates of the lth observation station ql
are assumed to be a prior, which can be measured by optical
instruments. Along the trajectory of the moving emitter, L
observation stations intercept the radiated signal at K short
intervals simultaneously and the time interval for adjacent
interceptions is set as T0 seconds.

C. Complexity Analysis and Implementation Suggestions
Since the cost function in L3(ξ) involves six unknown
parameters and requires Eigen-decomposition of the matrix,
it is challenging to obtain an analytic solution for the inter-
ested trajectory parameter vector. Here, the optimization is
performed by a grid search, which requires a high compu-
tation load. Specifically, the decomposition of ¯Qk involves
L3 multiplications. Since Uk is a N × L matrix, the num-
ber of multiplications for evaluating the Hermitian matrix
¯Qk = UkHUk is 0.5N L2. For each column of the matrix
Uk, FFT can be applied to calculate HH
l,kyl,k, which involves
2M N + 2Nlog2N + N multiplications. Since there are L
columns in Uk, the number of multiplications should be multi-
plied by L, i.e., L(2M N+2Nlog2N+N). Besides, considering
there are K matrix ¯Qk, the number of multiplications for
each grid is K(2M N L + 2N Llog2N + N L + 0.5N L2 + L3).
Therefore, the total number of multiplications for NG grids
is NG K(2M N L + 2N Llog2N + N L + 0.5N L2 + L3) ∼=
NG K N L(2M + 2log2N + 1 + 0.5L).
The grid size and grid number are essential for the grid
search method. Here, we provide a description for construct-
ing the parameter search space of interest. First, by using
the localization results from the traditional two-step method,
we can provide a rough estimate of the interested parameters.
Subsequently, we can further assess the quality of the received
signals, thereby determining the confidence level of the rough
estimate. Specifically, the SNR can be estimated by spectrum
analyzing. Utilizing the estimated SNR along with the roughly
estimated parameters, the CRBs of the interested parameters
can be further calculated to limit the range of the grid search
space. Following the method used in [39], for each interested
parameter, the grid size can be defined as a fraction of the
root of the corresponding CRB value.
Evidently, a smaller grid size leads to higher estimation
accuracy, but also results in increased computational burden.
To address this tradeoff, a combined approach using both
coarse and fine grid search can be employed to mitigate the
issue of the increased computational demands. Moreover, with
the development of powerful parallel computation platforms
such as graphics processing units (GPUs) and multicore pro-
cessors [40], [41], these techniques can also be implemented
in parallel efficiently to achieve superior performance.

V. NUMERICAL SIMULATION AND ANALYSIS
In this section, extensive simulations are performed to eval-
uate the proposed method and verify the theoretical analysis.
Four observation stations are placed separately at the corners
of a square with each side of 1 km. Each observation station
has a circular antenna array with five elements spaced evenly.
Fig. 2. Simulated location scenario.
The radius of the circle is set as half of the wavelength. The
moving emitter transmits a communication signal modulated
with binary phase shift keying (BPSK). The wavelength of the
radiated signal is 0.1 m, and its baud rate is Rb = 1 MHz.
The radiated signal is sampled at a rate of 1 MHz and
intercepted at different numbers of intervals along the emitter’s
trajectory. The time interval between adjacent interceptions is
T0 = 0.3 s. Unless otherwise specified, these parameter values
are consistent throughout all the simulations. The simulated
location scenario is depicted in Fig. 2.
A. Performance Evaluation Versus Different SNRs
In this section, we investigate the robustness of the proposed
method to different levels of measurement noise.
For comparison, two methods are selected as baselines to
evaluate the performance of the proposed method. Among
the traditional two-step approaches, we employ the maxi-
mum likelihood estimator due to its asymptotic efficiency.
We first extract the location measurements of AOA, TDOA,
and FDOA in each interval from the received signal via
MUSIC algorithm [9] and complex cross function [11], and
then use a weighted least squares to locate the emitter [17].
Note that the optimal weighted factors, related to the covari-
ance of the extracted measurements, are usually unavailable.
Therefore, we resort to the extracted measurements to estimate
the weighted factors [42]. The second baseline we choose
is the DPD-based method in [25], owing to its comparable
location scenario with that addressed in this study. Besides
these competitive baselines, the theoretical CRB also serves
as a benchmark to assess the performance of the proposed
method.

VI. CONCLUSION
In this study, we addressed the problem of trajectory esti-
mation for a 3-D moving emitter in a single step, in which
multiple widely distributed passive observation stations are
deployed to intercept the radiated signals. The proposed
method jointly employs the potential information of AOA,
TDOA, and FDOA embedded in the received signals based
on the maximum likelihood criterion. The optimal trajectory
parameters are selected as the candidates which can best
explain all the received signals in probabilistic senses. In addi-
tion, the performance analysis of station location errors is
achieved for moving emitters based on first-order Taylor series
approximation, and analytic relationships between location
errors and drifts in the trajectory parameters are provided with
closed-form expressions. Numerical simulations showed that
the proposed method could achieve superior performance in
trajectory parameters estimation and was statistically efficient.
The performance analysis for location errors was confirmed
with the simulated results, which enabled us to quantify the
effects of station location errors not only on the emitter
position but also on the moving direction. In our future
work, we will pay more attention to multiple-target loca-
tion scenarios and develop a more computationally efficient
localization algorithm. In addition, investigating an adaptive
framework that enables dynamic adjustment of algorithm
parameters based on real-time data to accommodate vari-
ous motion models is a challenging yet practical research
direction.
