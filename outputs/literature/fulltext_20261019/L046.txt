Performance analysis and improvement of direct position
determination based on Doppler frequency shifts in
presence of model errors: case of known waveforms
Ding Wang1,2 · Jiexin Yin1,2 · Ruirui Liu1,2 ·
Hongyi Yu1,2 · Yunlong Wang1,2
Received: 2 December 2016 / Revised: 15 March 2018 / Accepted: 12 April 2018 /
Published online: 26 April 2018
© Springer Science+Business Media, LLC, part of Springer Nature 2018
Abstract The direct position determination (DPD) method based on Doppler frequency
shifts for signals with known waveforms is ﬁrst proposed by Amar and Weiss. Although this
method exhibits excellent asymptotic performance, but does not account for the effects of
uncertainties in the receiver positions and velocities. These uncertainties may result in a con-
siderable reduction of localization accuracy. In this paper, the statistical performance of the
DPD estimator is investigated under receiver position and velocity errors (also called model
errors). We derive an analytical expression for the mean square error in the estimated source
location in the case where the estimator assumes that the receiver positions and velocities are
accurate, but they in fact contain errors. The main difﬁculty in the mathematical analysis is
that the DPD cost function is not explicit with respect to the emitter position. Consequently,
some algebraic manipulation is required to derive closed-form expressions for the ﬁrst- and
second-order partial derivatives of the cost function. The Cramér–Rao bounds (CRBs) for
the target position estimation are also deduced in the presence and absence of model errors.
These CRBs provide insights into the effects of model errors on the localization accuracy.
Two improved DPD methods are developed based on our analysis results. The ﬁrst has lower
complexity than the common grid search, and the second exhibits increased robustness to
model errors. Simulation results support and corroborate the theoretical developments in this
paper.
Keywords Direct position determination (DPD) · Performance analysis · Model errors ·
Doppler frequency shifts · Known waveforms · Mean square error (MSE) · Cramér–Rao
bound (CRB)

1 Introduction
Emitter localization has received signiﬁcant attention in the ﬁeld of signal processing because
of its importance in radar, sonar, navigation, sensor networks, and wireless communications
(Pulford 2010; Gogineni and Nehorai 2011; Shen and Win 2010; Zhang et al. 2016). Gen-
erally, the traditional source localization technique is based on a two-step procedure. In the
ﬁrst step, the intermediate parameters of the transmitted signals (e.g., angle of arrival (AOA)
(Schmidt 1986; Kutluyil 2005), time of arrival (TOA) (Cheung et al. 2004; Beck et al. 2008),
time difference of arrival (TDOA) (Yang and Ho 2009; Ho and Yang 2008), frequency of
arrival (FOA) (Mason 2004), frequency difference of arrival (FDOA) (Ho et al. 2007; Sun and
Ho 2011), received signal strength (RSS) (Rahman and Kleeman 2009), gain ratio of arrival
(GROA) (Ho and Sun 2008)) are measured at each station independently. In the second step,
the estimated parameters are used to determine the location of the emitter. This two-step
procedure is also known as the decentralized approach (Wax and Kailath 1985).
Although two-step localization is extensively used in modern localization systems, it
cannot achieve the minimum estimation variance. Indeed, this localization method is sub-
optimal, as the parameter estimation in the ﬁrst phase is performed independently by each
sensor, without using the constraint that all the observations relate to the same emitter loca-
tion. Recently, direct position determination (DPD) has emerged as a promising technique for
the localization of radio frequency sources (Amar and Weiss 2008; Weiss 2011; Li et al. 2016;
Pourhomayoun and Fowler 2014; Weiss 2004; Weiss and Amar 2005; Amar and Weiss 2007;
Oispuu and Nickel 2010; Bar-Shalom and Weiss 2009; Reuven and Weiss 2009). Although
this kind of localization method may require more computations and communication band-
width, novel information technology (Shen et al. 2017; Fu et al. 2016; Sun and Gu 2017)
can be used to overcome these difﬁculties. For example, the cloud computing and cloud
storage technology (Shen et al. 2017; Fu et al. 2016) can be used to reduce the computation
loads, and the compressive sensing technology (Sun and Gu 2017) is helpful for reducing
the communication bandwidth.
Indeed, DPD is a centralized and single-step estimation technique in which the estimator
uses exactly the same data as the classical two-step methods but determines the source
location directly. In general, the DPD method outperforms conventional two-step methods in
low signal-to-noise (SNR) conditions and when there are relatively few sample; moreover,
it does not encounter the association problem. More importantly, the DPD technique can be
applied to many wireless positioning systems. DPD methods have been proposed for locating
a narrowband radio emitter based on Doppler frequency shifts (Amar and Weiss 2008) and
for wideband signals using both the Doppler effects and relative delays (Weiss 2011; Li et al.
2016; Pourhomayoun and Fowler 2014). In addition, DPD methods for radio signals with
antenna arrays have been developed (Weiss 2004; Weiss and Amar 2005; Amar and Weiss
2007; Oispuu and Nickel 2010). As the temporal characteristics of signals can be exploited
in a more convenient way in DPD, methods for orthogonal frequency division multiplexing
signals (Bar-Shalom and Weiss 2009) and cyclostationary signals (Reuven and Weiss 2009)
have been proposed. Additionally, several DPD methods for locating transmitted signals with
known waveforms have been reported (Amar and Weiss 2008; Li et al. 2016; Weiss 2004;
Weiss and Amar 2005), and these achieve higher localization accuracy than methods that do
not exploit the signal waveform information. Applications of such studies can be found in,
for example, cooperative communication systems where some known preamble sequences
are transmitted for training purposes (Li et al. 1995; Gu et al. 2008).
Similar to traditional two-step localization, the localization accuracy of the DPD methods
may be degraded by model uncertainties. The model mismatches come from various sources.

For example, there may be uncertainties in the receiver locations and velocities, array cali-
bration errors, as well as scattering and multipath errors. The statistical performance of the
DPD approach in the presence of array model errors has been examined for the case of known
waveforms (Amar and Weiss 2006). The estimation accuracy of the DPD method presented in
Amar and Weiss (2008), which uses Doppler frequency shifts, has been rigorously analyzed
in the case of receiver position and velocity errors (Ding and Ying 2017). In this paper, our
theoretical derivation focuses on an alternative DPD method in Amar and Weiss (2008) that
assumes the signal waveforms are known. An analytical expression for the mean square error
of the source position estimate is derived when the estimator assumes the model errors do
not exist, but are in fact present. Note that the model errors herein refer to the random pertur-
bations in receiver positions and velocities. The theoretical formula for the increase in mean
square error (MSE) of the source location estimates resulting from random receiver loca-
tion and velocity perturbations is derived. The main difﬁculty in the mathematical analysis
stems from the fact that the DPD cost function in the estimator is not expressed analytically
with respect to the emitter position parameters. As a consequence, some algebraic manipu-
lations must be used to derive closed-form expressions for the ﬁrst- and second-order partial
derivatives of the cost function. Furthermore, the Cramér–Rao bounds (CRBs) for the source
position estimation are deduced for two cases: (a) the model errors are not present, and (b)
the model errors exist. Finally, we present two novel DPD methods on the basis of the perfor-
mance analysis results in this paper. Their excellent performances are veriﬁed by a number
of simulations.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Sect. 2, the signal model for DPD
is formulated. Section 3 brieﬂy describes the DPD estimator proposed in Amar and Weiss
(2008), which is suitable for the case of known waveforms. The statistical assumption and
effects of the model errors are discussed in Sect. 4. In Sect. 5, the analytical formula for
the MSE of the DPD method is derived in the presence of model errors. Section 6 derives
the CRB of the source location estimate for two cases: when model errors are absent and
when they are present. In Sect. 7, two improved DPD methods are developed. Section 8
presents numerical results that conﬁrm our theoretical developments. Conclusions are drawn
in Sect. 9. The proofs of the main results are shown in the appendixes.
2 Signal model for direct position determination
Consider a stationary radio emitter and L mobile receivers, synchronized in frequency and
time. The receivers intercept the radiating signal at K short intervals along their trajectory.
The emitter position is denoted by the D × 1 vector of coordinates q. Moreover, let pl,k
and ˙pl,k denote the coordinate and velocity vector of the lth receiver at the kth interception
interval, respectively, both of which are D × 1 vectors. Note that D denotes the coordinate
dimension, namely, D  2 for planar localization, and D  3 for three-dimensional (3D)
localization. For simplicity, only two-dimensional (2D) localization scenario, which is the
case studied in Amar and Weiss (2008), Weiss (2011), Li et al. (2016), Pourhomayoun and
Fowler (2014), Weiss (2004), Weiss and Amar (2005), Amar and Weiss (2007), is considered
here. The reason is that the extension of the theoretical developments in this paper to 3D space
is very straightforward. Additionally, when we consider 2D localization, the source position
vector q is given by q  [x y]T, where x and y denote x- and y-coordinates, respectively.
For the sake of clarity, Fig. 1 depicts two different geometrical conﬁgurations of the
problem in 2D, where both the receivers and the source are located in the x–y plane. The solid
lines show the receivers’ trajectories where the triangles indicate the interception intervals.

made.
Assumption 1 At each interception point, the observation time is sufﬁciently short that the
locations and velocities of the receivers remain unchanged during the data collection period.

time between the receivers, i.e., B < 1/τmax, where τmax is the maximal propagation time
between the receivers. As a result, the signal complex envelope is the same at all spatially

9 Conclusions
In this paper, the theoretical performance of the DPD estimator presented in Amar and Weiss
(2008) for signals with known waveforms is studied in the presence of receiver position and
velocity errors (also called model errors). We derive a closed-form expression for the MSE
in source location estimate when the estimator assumes the model errors do not exist, but are
in fact present. The main difﬁculty in the mathematical analysis stems from the fact that the
DPD cost function in the estimator is not explicit with respect to the emitter position. As a
result, some algebraic manipulations are used to deduce closed-form expressions for the ﬁrst-
and second-order partial derivatives of the cost function. The CRBs for the target position
estimation are deduced in both the absence and presence of model errors. Additionally, two
improved DPD methods are presented based on the performance analysis results in this paper.
The ﬁrst method consumes less computation and the second method is more robust to model
errors. Simulation results support and corroborate the validation of the theoretical analysis
as well as the advantage of the proposed DPD methods.
